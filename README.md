# GRC Policy Development | Securyth Consulting


## Objective

As a GRC analyst for Securyth Consulting, a simulated financial consulting firm, I developed 10 foundational security policies from scratch. The goal was to build a formal GRC framework that could manage organizational risk, meet ISO 27002 requirements, and protect sensitive client data.


## Scope

The project covered the entire organization. Three policies got special attention based on where the actual risk was: Remote Work, Mobile Devices, and Corporate Travel — areas where a mobile workforce creates the most exposure.


## Tools Used

| Tool / Framework | Purpose |
|---|---|
| ISO 27002:2022 | Primary control framework for drafting and mapping all policies |
| Wizer Platform | Policy distribution and employee acknowledgement tracking |
| Microsoft Office Suite | Policy drafting and compliance framework development |
| Simulated Environment | Scoped for Securyth Consulting, a financial firm with a highly mobile workforce |


## Case Background

Securyth Consulting had no formal, documented security policies. That gap left the firm exposed on three fronts: operational risk, regulatory risk, and reputational risk.

The business model made this worse. Consultants regularly work from home, travel to client sites, and handle confidential financial data outside of any controlled office environment. The firm needed policies that matched that reality, not generic templates written for a 9-to-5 desk job.

My job was to analyze the firm's actual risk profile, write a policy framework tied to ISO 27002, and build a system that would keep those policies active and enforced, not just filed away.



## Methodology

I worked through a structured GRC lifecycle from start to finish.

**Risk Assessment and Prioritization**
I started by mapping how the firm operates. The mobility of the workforce came up immediately as the biggest risk area. That is why Remote Work, Mobile Device, and Corporate Travel were built first and with the most detail.

**Policy Drafting and Framework Alignment**
I wrote 10 policies, each covering a specific security domain. Every policy statement was mapped back to a named ISO 27002:2022 control so the framework is auditable and traceable.

**Stakeholder Review and Approval**
Each draft went through a formal review process. Final sign-off came from executive leadership, including the Chief Information Officer. That top-down approval matters because it gives the policies actual weight inside the organization.

**Compliance Framework Development**
Writing policies is half the job. I built a Policy Compliance Framework in a spreadsheet to handle the other half. It assigns an owner to every policy, sets review schedules, and gives the organization a paper trail of compliance activity before an auditor ever shows up.

**Communication and Attestation**
I uploaded all 10 approved policies to Wizer. Every employee could access them in one place, and the platform tracked who read and acknowledged their responsibilities. That acknowledgement record is what turns a policy document into an enforceable obligation.

## Conclusion

This project built a working GRC foundation for Securyth Consulting. Ten ISO 27002-aligned policies. A compliance monitoring system. A formal communication plan. The firm went from no documented security governance to an auditable, structured framework.

**Impact:** The policy framework reduces regulatory and legal exposure, standardizes how security is handled across the organization, and gives the firm something concrete to show regulators and clients during audits.

**Recommendations:** My final report made one thing clear: policies need to be treated as living documents. I recommended embedding them into actual workflows, like access approval systems and onboarding checklists, scheduling reviews after major incidents or regulatory changes, and using the compliance tracker as a direct input into the internal audit program.


## Lessons Learned

This project taught me that the compliance framework is not a bonus deliverable. It is as important as the policies themselves. A policy with no enforcement mechanism is just a suggestion.

I also learned that good policy writing starts with understanding the business, not with reading a control framework. Once I understood that Securyth runs on mobility and client-site work, the decisions about what to prioritize, and how to write requirements that were actually usable, became much cleaner.

That combination of risk thinking and practical policy writing is what this project was really about.
